# Latent Language & the English-Pivot Hypothesis — Literature Review

Compiled 24 July 2026. Working reference for the gemma-2-2b logit-lens replication.

---

## 1. Source paper

**Wendler, C., Veselovsky, V., Monea, G., & West, R. (2024). Do Llamas Work in English? On the Latent Language of Multilingual Transformers.** ACL 2024, pp. 15366–15394. arXiv:2402.10588v4. Code: `epfl-dlab/llm-latent-language`.

### 1.1 Question

Do multilingual models trained on English-dominated corpora use English as an *implicit* internal pivot? Prior work had used English as an *explicit* pivot (translate in, process, translate out) with good results. The paper asks whether the same thing happens internally, unprompted.

### 1.2 Models

Llama-2 7B / 13B / 70B — 32 / 40 / 80 layers, d = 4096 / 5120 / 8192, vocab 32,000, run in 8-bit quantization. Llama-2's training corpus is 89.70% English; German is 0.17% (~3.4B tokens) and Chinese 0.13% (~2.6B tokens), so the model is English-dominated but genuinely multilingual in absolute token count.

Appendix B.2 adds Mistral-7B and reports consistent results, which is the paper's only evidence outside the Llama family.

### 1.3 Tasks

Three text-completion tasks, all few-shot, all designed so the correct next token is both obvious and unambiguously attributable to one language.

| Task | Form | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Translation | 4 demonstration pairs, 5th word left blank | semantic |
| Repetition | copy the word back, same language both sides | syntactic |
| Cloze | fill the masked word in a sentence (English sentences generated by GPT-4, then translated) | semantic |

### 1.4 Languages and word selection

Chinese is the primary language, chosen because it has many single-token words and no spaces. Robustness runs cover German, French, Russian; Estonian appears in Appendix B.1 as a low-resource case.

Counts: 139 Chinese, 104 German, 56 French, 115 Russian words.

**Single-token fractions matter more than the word counts.** Reported as total (single-token):

| | translation | repetition / cloze |
|---|---|---|
| zh | 353 (353) — 100% | 139 (139) — 100% |
| fr | 162 (88) — 54% | 56 (31) — 55% |
| de | 287 (126) — 44% | 104 (45) — 43% |
| ru | 324 (45) — 14% | 115 (15) — 13% |
| et | — | 1 of 99 |

### 1.5 How language probability is actually computed (Appendix A.2)

This is the methodological detail most likely to be got wrong in a replication.

P(lang = ℓ) is **not** the probability of one token. It is the sum over `Start(w)` — the set of every vocabulary token that could begin the correct word in language ℓ. That set includes:

- every token-level prefix of the word,
- the same prefixes with a leading space,
- for Chinese and Russian, tokenizations derived from the UTF-8 byte encodings of the characters.

Their worked example: for 花, P(ZH) sums the single token 花 and the leading byte token; for *flower*, P(EN) sums `f`, `fl`, `flow`, and the space-prefixed variants up to `_flower`.

Note that ∑ℓ P(lang = ℓ) < 1 — this is not a distribution over languages.

They also filter word lists to drop any target sharing a token prefix with its English version (e.g. French *photographier* vs English *photograph* both start `photo`), because that makes language attribution ambiguous.

### 1.6 Why they use logit lens and not tuned lens

Deliberate, and they say why: the tuned lens is trained to map intermediate states to the *final* next-token prediction. Since the final prediction is in the target language, training that mapping would optimize away the very English signal the study is trying to detect.

This is a reason not to add a tuned-lens comparison to a replication of this design.

### 1.7 Results — probabilistic view

Across model sizes, on translation and cloze:

1. No meaningful probability mass on either the target-language token or its English analog through the first half of layers. Entropy ~14 bits, near-uniform over the 32k vocab.
2. Around the middle layer, English rises sharply, then declines. Entropy drops hard at the same moment.
3. Target language grows slowly, crosses over English, and spikes in the last ~5 layers.

**On repetition the picture is weaker, and this is the part most replications misread.** In most languages the English-first pattern still appears, but it is less pronounced — the input language rises earlier than in the other tasks. For Chinese it rises simultaneously with or faster than English. Their explanation: recognising that the task is a copy still requires semantic understanding, which happens in concept space, which is English-biased. The Chinese exception is attributed to tokenization — where clean language-specific tokens exist, the English detour is less pronounced.

**Implication: the repetition task is not a null control.** It is a weaker-effect condition, not a no-effect condition.

### 1.8 Results — geometric view

The stronger half of the paper, run on 70B / translation only.

Because of RMS normalization, latents lie on a hypersphere of radius √d ≈ 90.1. Output token embeddings approximately lie on their own sphere, radius ~1.52 (SD 0.23).

They define **token energy** E(h)² — the mean squared cosine between a latent and the token embedding rows, normalised by the mean squared cosine among token embeddings themselves. It measures how much of a latent's magnitude is actually pointed at token space, i.e. how much of it the logit lens can see at all.

Three phases in the 70B forward pass:

| Phase | Layers | Entropy | Token energy | Language |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–40 | ~14 bits | low (~20%) | none |
| 2 | 41–70 | 1–2 bits | low | English |
| 3 | 71–80 | low | spikes to ~30% | Chinese |

### 1.9 The conceptual model, and the claim they actually make

Phase 1 builds a feature representation of the *current* token, largely orthogonal to output-token space. Phase 2 places latents in an abstract **concept space** that correlates with output tokens but carries much else besides — and within that space, English variants of a concept sit closer to the concept embedding than non-English variants, because of training exposure. Phase 3 rotates into the target language and discards prediction-irrelevant information, which is what spikes the energy.

Their conclusion is more careful than the title suggests: the internal lingua franca is **not English but concepts — concepts biased toward English**. English is a pivot in a semantic sense, not a lexical one. The model is not translating to English and restarting its forward pass.

### 1.10 Stated limitations

- Llama-2 family only; Mistral-7B is the sole out-of-family check.
- Closed-source models inaccessible by this method.
- Tasks are deliberately toy-like and single-token; a first demonstration of existence, not coverage.
- The structure of "concept space" in its native high dimension is not characterised.
- The logit lens is blind to whatever component of a latent is orthogonal to token space; everything else in the representation enters the analysis as noise.

## 2. Follow-up literature

### 2.1 The camp that says concepts are language-agnostic

**Dumas et al. (2024)** — activation patching / tracing rather than lens-based reading, finding further evidence for language-agnostic concept representations. Direct follow-up from the same group.

**Wu, Yu, Yogatama, Lu & Kim (2024). The Semantic Hub Hypothesis.** arXiv:2411.04986. Argues models share semantic representations not only across languages but across *modalities* — the broadest version of the shared-representation claim.

**Brinkmann et al. (2025)** — abstract grammatical concepts (number, gender, tense) are encoded in feature directions shared across typologically diverse languages, shown on Llama-3 and Aya-8B.

### 2.2 The camp that says the concept space is skewed to the dominant language

**Zhong, Cheng, Liu, Jiang, Wan, Chu, Murawaki & Kurohashi (2024). Beyond English-Centric LLMs: What Language Do Multilingual Language Models Think in?** arXiv:2408.10811.

The most directly relevant paper for a Japanese study. Compares three model types for Japanese: Llama-2 (English-centric), Swallow (English-centric plus continued Japanese pretraining), and LLM-jp (pretrained on balanced English + Japanese). Finding: Llama-2 uses English exclusively as internal latent language, while Swallow and LLM-jp show **dual** internal latent languages, and the model preferentially activates whichever latent language is closest to the target. Also probes what happens when latent and target language carry conflicting cultural content.

**Schut, Gal & Farquhar (2025). Do Multilingual LLMs Think In English?** arXiv:2502.15603.

Models: Llama-3.1-70B, Mixtral-8x22B, **Gemma-2-27B**, Aya-23-35B. Languages: French, German, Dutch, Mandarin. Findings:

- English-centric latent space **for semantically-loaded words**.
- The pattern **does not hold for non-lexical words** — explicitly at odds with Brinkmann.
- Activation steering works better when steering vectors are computed in English than in the input/output language — a causal result, not just a lens reading.
- Smaller models appear to share more representations across languages than larger ones.

**Fierro et al. (2025)** — subject enrichment during retrieval; cited by Schut in the dominant-language camp. *(Low confidence — I only have this secondhand from Schut's related-work section.)*

### 2.3 Adjacent / instrumental

- **Nostalgebraist (2020)** — original logit lens.
- **Belrose et al. (2023)** — tuned lens. arXiv:2303.08112. Relevant as the thing Wendler deliberately declines.
- **Cancedda (2024)** — spectral filters, dark signals, attention sinks. arXiv:2402.09221. Cited by Wendler on the structure of token space; relevant background for token energy.
- **Do LLMs Need to Think in One Language? Correlation between Latent Language and Task Performance.** arXiv:2505.21458. Asks whether latent-language choice actually predicts downstream performance.
- **Tracing Multilingual Representations in LLMs with Cross-Layer Transcoders.** arXiv:2511.10840. Newer method applied to the same question.

## 3. Where the field actually disagrees

Two open seams, one of them directly reachable from an existing gemma-2-2b setup.

**Seam 1 — lexical vs grammatical content.** Schut et al. find the English-centric pattern holds for semantically-loaded words but not non-lexical ones. Brinkmann et al. find grammatical concepts *are* shared across languages. These are concurrent and in tension. Neither tested Japanese, which offers an unusually clean separation: particles (は / が / を) and counters (本 / 枚 / 匹) are grammatical machinery with no English lexical analog to pivot through, sitting in the same sentence as ordinary nouns that do have one.

**Seam 2 — model scale and family.** Schut reports that smaller models share more representations than larger ones. Their smallest is 27B. Wendler's smallest is 7B. The 2B range is untested, and Gemma-2-2B is not the same object as Gemma-2-27B.

**Not a seam:** whether the repetition control separates. Wendler already answered that (§1.7 above) — it mostly doesn't, and they explain why. A replication finding no separation is reproducing them, not contradicting them.

## 4. Consequences for this project

### 4.1 Three corrections

1. **Reframe the control.** A non-separating repetition control is a replication of Wendler §6, not an anomaly. The interesting question is whether the *degree* of separation tracks single-token fraction the way their cross-language comparison predicts.

2. **Fix the scoring before interpreting anything.** Current implementation scores a single first token per language. Wendler sums over `Start(w)` including byte-level fallbacks (§1.5). Japanese kanji in Gemma's SentencePiece vocab will hit byte fallback exactly as Chinese does in Llama-2's. This is the most likely cause of the near-zero Japanese curve in the repetition condition, where Japanese is the correct answer and should be high at the final layer.

3. **Do not add a tuned-lens comparison.** §1.6 — it would optimize away the signal. Stating this in the write-up demonstrates the paper was read properly.

### 4.2 The defensible claim

> Wendler et al. attribute the weak English-detour on Chinese repetition to tokenization. Japanese on gemma-2-2b tests that explanation on a language, model family, and parameter scale none of the follow-up work covers.

Modest, measured against a published baseline, and it lands inside Seam 1 rather than beside it.

### 4.3 Sequence

| Day | Work |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reimplement `Start(w)` scoring; re-run both conditions |
| 2 | Report single-token fraction for the Japanese word list, in the format of §1.4 |
| 3–4 | Lexical vs grammatical targets — nouns against particles / counters |
